Buying

Vehicle History Reports

A vehicle history report (such as CARFAX or AutoCheck) is the single best tool for avoiding a bad used-car purchase. It compiles records tied to the 17-character VIN.

What the Report Shows

  • Title brands: salvage, flood, rebuilt, or lemon
  • Reported accidents and repair records
  • Odometer readings and rollback flags
  • Number of owners and service history
  • Open recalls and registration states

Reading the VIN

The VIN identifies the exact vehicle. The first characters show the country and manufacturer; digits near the end identify the model year and plant. Always match the VIN on the report to the one stamped on the dashboard and door jamb.

Limits

Reports rely on reported data — a crash paid in cash may never appear. Use the report alongside a mechanic inspection, not instead of one.

Tip: Ask the seller to provide the report free; if they refuse, that is a red flag.

Why History Matters

A vehicle history report reveals accidents, flood damage, odometer rollbacks, and title brands that a clean exterior hides. A "salvage" or "rebuilt" brand means the car was declared a total loss — and may never be safe or insurable at a normal rate.

What to Pull

  • Title status and brand history
  • Reported accidents and airbag deployments
  • Number of prior owners and use type
  • Service and recall records where available

Limits of the Report

A clean report is not proof — unreported crashes and small-operator sales leave gaps. Always pair the report with a mechanic's inspection and a test drive before committing.

Recalls and Open Campaigns

Check the VIN for open recalls on the manufacturer site or NHTSA. A used car can carry an unfixed safety recall — free to fix at a dealer, but a real risk if ignored. Confirm the repair was actually completed.

Private vs. Dealer History

Dealer-certified cars include a inspection and often a warranty, reflected in price. Private sales skip both but cost less. Match the source to your risk tolerance and always inspect either way.

Mechanical Inspection

Pay a trusted mechanic for a pre-purchase inspection before you buy. For under a hundred dollars they can flag worn brakes, leak, or frame damage that a detail job hides. That fee prevents a thousand-dollar surprise.
